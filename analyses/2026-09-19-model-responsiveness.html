Does the model damp change?

Testing whether the three-term physics model under-predicts rises and over-predicts drops — and whether the bias you see first is real or an artefact of how you measure it.

No. The model tracks the scale of day-on-day change correctly. Regressing actual change on predicted change gives a slope of 1.047 ± 0.041 — statistically indistinguishable from the ideal 1.0 (t = +1.16). The strong damping that appears on first inspection is a measurement-noise artefact, not a model failure.

Why the first panel lies

Residual is defined as predicted_change − actual_change. Bucketing by actual change therefore sorts on a quantity that appears, negated, inside the residual itself. Any measurement error in the day's reading pushes a leg into an extreme bucket and pushes its residual the opposite way, so the pattern appears even for a perfect model.

Predicted change carries no such shared term with the day's measurement. Bucketing on it is the honest test, and it clears the model.

Does training on longer gaps fix it?

The idea: a 2- or 3-day leg carries the same thermometer error over a larger real change, so noise should matter less and the fit should stop hedging. Expanding the training set from 313 one-day legs to 933 legs across horizons 1–3 tests it. Both models are then scored on the same 313 one-day legs, so only the coefficients differ.

Better

Trained on 1-day legs only

Slope 1.010 · k_air 0.007696 · RMSE 0.3575

Worse

Trained on 1–3 day legs

Slope 1.049 · k_air 0.007394 · RMSE 0.3583

It makes calibration worse, not better. Adding the longer legs pulls k_air down (0.007696 → 0.007394), which makes the model less responsive, which lifts the slope further above 1.0. RMSE is flat — 0.3575 against 0.3583 — matching the earlier finding in PR #56 that multi-horizon training buys nothing on error.

The premise doesn’t hold because a 3-day leg is not an average of three daily readings. It has exactly one endpoint measurement, carrying the full thermometer error, while the model must compound three days of weather to reach it. Longer legs add compounding misspecification faster than they add signal, and the fit compromises toward coefficients that are wrong for one day.

What this does and doesn't settle

  • Settled: the model is not systematically damping. Direction and scale of day-on-day change are right, and large moves are tracked as well as small ones.
  • Unexplained spread: actual changes vary 27% more than predicted ones (sd 0.632 vs 0.499 °C). With the slope at 1.0, that is noise and missing physics — not shrinkage.
  • One live oddity: legs where the model predicted a −0.2 to −0.4 °C drop actually fell −0.455 °C, a +0.159 °C bias on n=38. The only bucket that doesn't clear. Possibly noise; worth a second look.
  • Ruled out: training on 2–3 day gaps. It lowers k_air and worsens the slope to 1.049, with RMSE flat.
  • Still open: a December bias of +0.24 °C, and no wind or evaporation term in the model — the likeliest candidate for both that and the extra spread.
